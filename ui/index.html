    Guide

    How an end grain board goes together

    1. Start with a rough or sawn board, a little bigger than you need.

    2. Rip it into strips and plane them to size.

    3. Glue the strips edge to edge into a panel, then flatten it.

    4. Crosscut the panel into slices. Each cut loses a kerf.

    5. Stand the slices end grain up and glue them side by side. Each slice is one column of the board.

    Columns or rows. The pictures show each slice becoming a column. The slices can just as well become the board's rows: choose under the board picture, at "Crosscut the first glue-up into". Then each strip makes a column instead of a row, and wherever this guide says column, read row.

    End Grain Calc works all of this out from the board you draw: how much timber to buy, how to cut it into strips, how to glue the strips into panels, and how to cut and arrange the slices. The other pages of this guide say how.

    Designing a board

    The board is a grid of rows and columns, seen from above. Set its finished width, depth and thickness, and how many columns and rows it has, under "Finished board" on the left. The − and + buttons take away or add a column or row at the end.

    Paint
    Pick a timber in the list, then click a cell, or drag across a run of them. Keys 1 to 9 pick the first nine timbers.
    Size
    Click a row or column on the yellow tape and type its size, or drag the join between two of them. In any size box the up and down arrow keys change it a step at a time, and Enter keeps what you typed.
    Move
    Drag a row or column along the tape and drop it where you want it.
    Pinned and not pinned
    A size you type or drag is pinned: it stays put whatever else changes, and the tape shows it in bold on a darker band with a pin. The rest are not pinned and share what is left of the board equally, so they stretch or shrink when the board does. Each row's or column's box has a Not pinned | Pinned switch, and "Make all equal" unpins every one.
    Row and column boxes
    Clicking the tape opens a small box for that row or column. As well as its size, it can paint the whole row or column in the timber you have picked, add one either side, or remove it.
    Undo and redo
    The two arrow buttons in the header, or Cmd+Z and Shift+Cmd+Z (Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Shift+Z on Windows and Linux). They cover every change to the board.
    Millimetres or inches
    The mm/in switch in the header changes this board's units and converts every size. Settings chooses what new boards start in. In millimetres every size is a whole millimetre, except the saw kerf.
    When it doesn't add up
    A note above the board says what is wrong and offers a fix: pinned sizes that leave no room for the rest, say, or unpinned ones that would come to a fraction of a millimetre.

    Timbers

    Each timber's ··· button opens its editor: its colour, its price by the board foot with the thickness to buy it as, and the sawn sizes your mill sells it in with their prices per metre. "Add a timber" adds one of your own, and "Remove this timber" is at the top of its editor.

    Saving and your boards

    Save board (or Cmd+S, Ctrl+S on Windows and Linux) saves the board under its name, which you can change at the top of the window. "Not saved" shows beside the name while there are changes to save. Boards lists your saved boards to open or delete, and has "New board" and "Duplicate this board". The app asks before closing, or opening another board, with unsaved changes.

    The lists

    The right-hand side changes as you design. It is what you take to the mill and into the workshop.

    The summary
    At the top: how much timber to order, and what it will cost.
    Order from the mill
    What to buy, with the cost. The Ordering page explains it. "Copy order" copies it as plain text, ready for an email or a message to the mill.
    Milled cutting list
    Every strip with its finished thickness, width and length, and the panel it goes into. Under each is the oversize piece to cut first: the width to rip it at and the length to cut it, which leaves your allowances for planing. "On edge" means the strip's width comes out of the stock's thickness.
    Glue-ups
    A drawing of each panel. The strips are numbered in gluing order, with the timber and finished width of each beside its number. Red lines are saw cuts and the hatched ends are waste. The drawing is the way the board faces: strips run left to right when slices become columns, and top to bottom when they become rows. If a drawing is stretched so every strip can be labelled, it says so.
    Order of work
    The steps from the mill to the finished board, with this board's own sizes.

    Columns or rows

    Under the board picture, Crosscut the first glue-up into chooses whether each slice becomes a column or a row of the board. "Whichever needs fewer" picks the way with fewer glue-ups; each button says how many its way needs.

    Printing

    Print these lists, at the top of the right-hand side, prints the board with all of the lists: the picture and the summary share the top of the first page and the lists run on from there. On a Mac, File > Print Lists (Cmd+P) does the same. Print this guide, at the top of this guide, prints every page of it.

    Ordering timber

    The order has a switch for the two ways mills sell hardwood.

    By the board foot
    Rough stock by timber and thickness (4/4, 8/4 and so on), priced per board foot, with your extra for defects added. Each timber is kept to one stock thickness unless mixing two saves more than a tenth. To always buy a timber in one thickness, set "Buy as" in its editor.
    By sawn size
    For mills that sell fixed sawn sections by the metre, such as 50 × 150 mm. The order says which sections to buy and how many metres of each. Under each line is "Ask the mill for": the lengths to ask for, rounded up to the step the mill sells in and including your extra for defects, so they add up to the metres ordered. Then come the cuts: how many pieces to crosscut and how long, and what to rip each into. A chip such as 2 ×rip51 mm→ plane45 mm means two strips, each ripped at 51 mm and planed to 45 mm.

    On a sawn order

    • A timber with no sawn sizes entered stays on a board-foot list beneath.
    • A strip too big for every sawn size its timber comes in is left off, with the reason and the largest size that would fit. Tick "Order these by the board foot" to buy those that way instead.
    • Avoid ripping gives a strip a whole sawn board to itself, planed to size, where that would plane away no more than the share of the board you set (half, to start with). Thinner strips are still ripped from wider boards. The note beside it says how many strips that affects and what it does to the cost.
    • The step the mill sells length in (0.3 m to start with, or your price list's) is under the order. Tax, at the rate you set, is added at the bottom.

    Longest board I can carry

    Under "Saw and allowances". Nothing on the order, and no strip, is longer than this: glue-ups are split into shorter panels if their strips would be too long. Type it as 2.4m, 8ft or in the board's own units, or leave it empty for no limit.

    No price yet

    Until a timber has a price, the order says "add a price" beside it rather than showing a cost of nothing. Click it to open that timber's editor.

    Your mill's prices

    The app starts with no prices, because every mill's prices and sizes are different. There are two ways to give it yours.

    Type them in
    Open a timber's ··· editor and enter its price per board foot, or the sawn sizes your mill sells it in with the price per metre of each.
    Import a whole price list
    Use Import a price list, under the timber list or in Settings:
    1. "Copy the prompt" and give it to an AI assistant that can read web pages, along with your mill's website. The prompt tells it what to look for and how to set it out.
    2. Paste what it gives you into the box, or choose the file, and press "Read this price list".
    3. Tick the timbers you use. Their prices go onto your timber list, and any you don't have yet are added.

    An assistant can misread a page, so check a few prices against the mill's own list. Prices already on your timbers stay as they are until you tick them again.

    Once a list is imported, a timber that has no sawn sizes yet gets a button on the order to use the mill's prices for it. A price list is a small file; the README on GitHub describes it.

    How the numbers are worked out

    The rules below are written for slices as columns; for rows, swap the two words. Every allowance named here is under "Saw and allowances" on the left, is saved with the board, and goes back to its starting value with "Reset allowances".

    Panels
    Columns with the same width and the same run of timbers come from one panel. A column that is another one upside down shares its panel too (the slice is turned round), provided the rows are symmetrical. That is how a checkerboard comes from a single glue-up.
    Strip length
    Slices × (board thickness + end grain flattening), plus a kerf for every cut, plus the panel end waste. Never less than the shortest strip you allow.
    Strip thickness and width
    Thickness is the column's width plus the panel flattening. Width is the row's size, plus the edge trim on the two outer strips if you set one.
    Rough stock
    Each strip's size plus the rough thickness, width and length losses, and one kerf on the width for the rip. A strip is taken flat or on edge, whichever uses less timber. Board feet use the nominal thickness, so 8/4 counts as 2 inches.
    Rip at
    Strips are ripped oversize and planed down. The width to rip at is the finished width plus the rough width loss, and it is shown beside every finished size.
    Whole millimetres
    On a board in millimetres every allowance except the saw kerf is kept to a whole millimetre, and strip lengths are rounded up to one, so no size to cut has a decimal.
    Extra for defects
    Added to the order for knots, checks and sapwood: a fifth to start with.

    Glossary

    Row, column, cell
    Seen from above, the finished board is a grid of rows and columns. A cell is one block of end grain where a row and a column cross.
    Strip
    A long piece of one timber, planed square. When slices become columns, its width is one row of the board and its thickness is one column. When slices become rows, it is the other way round.
    Panel
    The first glue-up: strips glued edge to edge. One panel supplies every column (or every row, if slices become rows) that has the same width and the same run of timbers.
    Slice
    A piece crosscut off the end of a panel, a little longer than the board is thick. Stood on end, it becomes one column of the board, or one row if you crosscut into rows.
    Slices as columns or rows
    The choice under the board picture. It decides which way the first glue-up is made. "Whichever needs fewer" picks the way with fewer glue-ups: a board of plain stripes needs one glue-up one way and several the other.
    Kerf
    The width of the saw cut. It is lost at every rip and every crosscut, so it is added between strips and between slices.
    Pinned and unpinned
    A pinned row or column keeps the size you typed, and the tape shows it in bold on a darker band with a pin. Unpinned ones share whatever is left over equally, so they stretch or shrink with the board.
    Rip at
    The width to set the saw to. It is the finished size plus your rough width loss, so there is timber left to plane.
    On edge
    The strip is cut so that its width comes out of the board’s thickness rather than across its face. It lets a thinner board make a wider strip.
    Rough losses
    What planing takes off sawn timber to make it flat and square. Thickness, width and length each have their own allowance.
    Flattening
    Extra left on the strips (panel flattening) and on the slices (end grain flattening) so each glue-up can be planed or sanded flat afterwards.
    Edge trim
    Extra width on the two outer strips, cut away when the finished board is squared up.
    Board foot
    A volume of timber: 1 inch thick, 12 inches wide and 12 inches long. Rough thickness is given in quarter inches, so 8/4 is 2 inches.
    Sawn size
    A fixed cross-section that a mill sells by the metre, such as 50 × 150 mm. Sizes are nominal and can vary by a millimetre or two.
    Avoid ripping
    Gives a strip a whole sawn board to itself, planed to size, where that would not waste too much of the board.

    About End Grain Calc

    Check the numbers before you buy timber or cut it. End Grain Calc is a free tool and it can be wrong, and your saw, your timber and your mill will not match its assumptions exactly. It comes with no warranty, and the author takes no responsibility for errors or for anything that follows from using it.

    Version
    This is version …. When the app starts it asks GitHub which version is the latest, and shows a small button in the header if a newer one is out. That question is all the app sends over the internet, and nothing about you or your boards goes with it. Turn it off under Settings. Every version is on the releases page.
    Where your work is kept
    On this computer, in your home folder, in .endgrain: a file for each board in boards, and your timber list, settings and mill's price list beside it. They are plain text files, so they are easy to back up or copy to another computer.
