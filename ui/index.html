    How an end grain board goes together

    1. Start with a rough or sawn board, a little bigger than you need.

    2. Rip it into strips and plane them to size.

    3. Glue the strips edge to edge into a panel, then flatten it.

    4. Crosscut the panel into slices. Each cut loses a kerf.

    5. Stand the slices end grain up and glue them side by side. Each slice is one column of the board.

    Columns or rows. The pictures show each slice becoming a column. The slices can just as well become the board's rows: choose under the board picture, at "Crosscut the first glue-up into". Then each strip makes a column instead of a row, and wherever this guide says column, read row.

    What the words mean

    Row, column, cell
    Seen from above, the finished board is a grid of rows and columns. A cell is one block of end grain where a row and a column cross.
    Strip
    A long piece of one timber, planed square. When slices become columns, its width is one row of the board and its thickness is one column. When slices become rows, it is the other way round.
    Panel
    The first glue-up: strips glued edge to edge. One panel supplies every column (or every row, if slices become rows) that has the same width and the same run of timbers.
    Slice
    A piece crosscut off the end of a panel, a little longer than the board is thick. Stood on end, it becomes one column of the board, or one row if you crosscut into rows.
    Slices as columns or rows
    The choice under the board picture. It decides which way the first glue-up is made. "Whichever needs fewer" picks the way with fewer glue-ups: a board of plain stripes needs one glue-up one way and several the other.
    Kerf
    The width of the saw cut. It is lost at every rip and every crosscut, so it is added between strips and between slices.
    Pinned and unpinned
    A pinned row or column keeps the size you typed, and the tape shows it in bold on a darker band with a pin. Unpinned ones share whatever is left over equally, so they stretch or shrink with the board.
    Rip at
    The width to set the saw to. It is the finished size plus your rough width loss, so there is timber left to plane.
    On edge
    The strip is cut so that its width comes out of the board’s thickness rather than across its face. It lets a thinner board make a wider strip.
    Rough losses
    What planing takes off sawn timber to make it flat and square. Thickness, width and length each have their own allowance.
    Flattening
    Extra left on the strips (panel flattening) and on the slices (end grain flattening) so each glue-up can be planed or sanded flat afterwards.
    Edge trim
    Extra width on the two outer strips, cut away when the finished board is squared up.
    Board foot
    A volume of timber: 1 inch thick, 12 inches wide and 12 inches long. Rough thickness is given in quarter inches, so 8/4 is 2 inches.
    Sawn size
    A fixed cross-section that a mill sells by the metre, such as 50 × 150 mm. Sizes are nominal and can vary by a millimetre or two.
    Avoid ripping
    Gives a strip a whole sawn board to itself, planed to size, where that would not waste too much of the board.
